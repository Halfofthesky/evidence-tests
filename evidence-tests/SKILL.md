# Evidence tests

Weigh the evidence for a claim about a particular case the way process tracing does. Ask of each piece of evidence two questions. Would it have to be there if the claim were true? Could anything else have put it there? The answers sort it into one of four tests. Then check that the claim is stated no more strongly than its tests allow, that the rival explanations are treated as fairly as the favoured one, and that missing evidence is read correctly.

Sources:
- Stephen Van Evera, *Guide to Methods for Students of Political Science* (Cornell University Press, 1997), 30–35.
- David Collier, "Understanding Process Tracing," *PS: Political Science & Politics* 44.4 (2011): 823–830, with the 2019 addendum.
- James Mahoney, "The Logic of Process Tracing Tests in the Social Sciences," *Sociological Methods & Research* 41.4 (2012): 570–597.
- Sherry Zaks, "Relationships Among Rivals (RAR): A Framework for Analyzing Contending Hypotheses in Process Tracing," *Political Analysis* 25.3 (2017): 344–362.
- Andrew Bennett and Jeffrey T. Checkel, eds., *Process Tracing: From Metaphor to Analytic Tool* (Cambridge University Press, 2015): ch. 1 (Bennett and Checkel), ch. 2 (Alan M. Jacobs, "Process tracing the effects of ideas"), and the Appendix (Bennett, "Disciplining our conjectures").

## When it applies

The claims this skill tests are about particular cases:
- causal claims: X caused Y, X contributed to Y, Y happened because of X;
- existence claims: an event, a belief or a process took place, even though no source records it directly (Mahoney treats these separately from causal claims);
- influence claims, common in history and the humanities: author A read B, an idea reached a decision-maker, a text shaped a policy. These combine both kinds. Jacobs: an idea must have been available to the actor said to have used it, which makes availability a hoop test.

Historical narrative often makes causal claims without saying so ("after the reform, the guilds declined"). Reconstruct them and label them as reconstructed. If the text makes no claims of these kinds, say the skill does not apply and stop. Interpretive claims about meaning ("the poem is ironic") are not causal claims. Do not force them into the tests; note it and ask.

## Modes

1. **Audit**: the user's own draft. Map the claims and evidence, classify, flag, and propose moves. Do not rewrite the text.
2. **Read**: someone else's text. Map and classify, and say how much the evidence supports each claim and against which rivals. No verdict on the work unless asked.
3. **Plan**: research not yet done. For the user's hypothesis and each rival, derive the evidence each predicts, and say in advance which test each prediction would make. This tells the user what to look for first.

Infer the mode from the input and state the assumption. Work on the one to three claims the argument rests on. If the user does not name them, pick them and say which ones you picked.

## Core principles

- **Two dimensions** (Van Evera). *Certitude*: how unequivocally the claim predicts the evidence, so that its absence would count against the claim. *Uniqueness*: how far no other known explanation predicts the same evidence, so that its presence counts for this claim. "Certainty and uniqueness are both matters of degree." Record both as high or low, with a one-line reason.
- **Uniqueness is relative to named rivals.** Evidence is unique only against particular alternatives. Say which.
- **The four types are a heuristic.** Collier: "a useful heuristic, but should not be taken rigidly." Which test a piece of evidence makes depends on the researcher's background knowledge, the study's assumptions and how the hypothesis is worded. State the assumption that puts each item in its type. Where the text supports two types, give both (flag E14).
- **Every test rests on a generalisation** (Mahoney). To call evidence necessary or sufficient for a claim is to assume some general rule about how such things happen. Name the rule. If it is only probabilistic, the test is a straw in the wind, whatever the text calls it. If the rule is wrong, the test is void.
- **Difficulty depends on how common the condition is** (Mahoney). A hoop test is hard to pass when the condition it requires is rare in that context, and a hard pass gives real support. It is trivial when the condition is nearly always present. A smoking-gun test is easy to fail when its condition is normal in that context, and failing an easy one counts against the claim.
- **Confidence must match the tests.** Words such as "shows", "proves", "must have" and "clearly" need at least one passed smoking-gun or doubly decisive test, or the elimination of every serious rival. Passed hoop tests keep a claim alive; they do not confirm it.
- **No invented numbers.** Do not attach probabilities or likelihood ratios unless the text supplies them. Bennett's Bayesian appendix explains *why* the tests work. Use its logic (how likely the evidence is if the claim is true, against how likely if it is false), not made-up figures.

## The four tests

Adapted from Van Evera (31–32) and Collier's Table 1, which builds on Bennett.

| Test | Certitude | Uniqueness | If passed | If failed | Rivals, if passed / if failed |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Straw in the wind | low | low | relevance affirmed, not confirmed | slightly weakened, not eliminated | slightly weakened / slightly strengthened |
| Hoop | high | low | relevance affirmed, not confirmed | eliminated | somewhat weakened / somewhat strengthened |
| Smoking gun | low | high | confirmed | somewhat weakened, not eliminated | substantially weakened / somewhat strengthened |
| Doubly decisive | high | high | confirmed, others eliminated | eliminated | eliminated / substantially strengthened |

Van Evera's examples: the accused was in the state on the day of the murder (hoop); a smoking gun seen in a suspect's hand just after the shooting (smoking gun); a bank camera that records the robbers' faces (doubly decisive, and rare); "If Hitler ordered the Holocaust, we should probably find some written record of his orders" (straw in the wind). Most evidence in real work is straw in the wind. The rivals column holds only for rivals that are mutually exclusive with the claim (see Rivals).

**Cumulation** (Bennett, Appendix). Many weak tests can add up to strong support, but only if they are independent of one another and point the same way. Evidence that follows from other evidence already counted adds almost nothing. Evidence that bears on only one rival loses force as it piles up; diverse evidence, bearing on different rivals, does more.

## Rivals

**Find them** (Bennett and Checkel, best practices 1–2). Cast the net widely:
- the explanations regional and functional specialists give for this case and for its class of cases;
- the explanations participants and journalists give, restated in theoretical terms;
- the major categories: material power, institutional constraints and opportunities, social norms or legitimacy;
- agent-based and structural explanations alike.

Label which rivals come from the text and which from your knowledge of the field. Your claims about the field are checkable; say what would check them.

**Treat them equally.** A rival need not get equal depth, but it must get equal toughness. Watch for "first mover advantage": the favoured explanation is traced in full, and rivals are brought in only to explain its anomalies. Counter-intuitive rivals, with a lower prior expectation of being true, may fairly need more evidence; say so when the text relies on this.

**Fix their relationship to the claim** (Zaks, Table 2 and decision tree). This decides whether evidence for one explanation counts against another.
- *Mutually exclusive*: both cannot hold in the case. This is the only relationship in which support for one undermines the other. It comes in three forms: *outcome* (the two predict divergent outcomes), *evidentiary* (one requires evidence that would undermine the other), and *conditional* (exclusive only under some scope condition). Zaks: "Mutual exclusivity is a strong modeling assumption for which the burden of proof is on researchers."
- *Coincident*: independent of each other; both may contribute, or each may operate in different cases.
- *Congruent*: they need similar evidence and may work together; support for one supports the other.
- *Inclusive*: a special case of congruence; one turns out to extend the other.

To decide, ask in order:
1. Do the two predict divergent outcomes? If yes, is the divergence produced by a scope condition or level of analysis? (yes: conditional; no: outcome exclusivity)
2. Do they require divergent evidence, meaning evidence that is *incompatible*, not merely *different*? Zaks warns that confusing different with divergent evidence causes many errors. Two congruent explanations may each need their own kind of evidence. Finding the kind one needs does not rule the other out.
3. If neither: coincident, congruent or inclusive, depending on whether support for one bears on the other.

**Elimination** (Collier, Holmes's method). A claim can be established by eliminating its rivals through failed hoop tests, while its own support stays weak. This works only if the rivals are mutually exclusive and the set of rivals is reasonably complete. Check both.

## Missing evidence

"Absence of evidence" counts as "evidence of absence" only when the evidence would have been produced, kept and found. Van Evera's dispute over the 1898 conspiracy theory turns on exactly this: the same empty archive is a failed hoop to one side and a mere straw to the other, because they disagree about certitude. Ask (Jacobs, ch. 2; Bennett and Checkel, ch. 1):
- Do we have records from the venues where the actors would have voiced or acted on the claim?
- How complete is that record?
- Did the actors have reasons to record or voice it, or reasons to hide it?
- Is the belief or act stigmatised? Evidence of stigmatised beliefs is harder to find than evidence of widely accepted ones.

Unavailable evidence (closed archives, classified files) limits how confident the conclusion can be. A prediction about what it will show, if later borne out, is strong support.

## Sources of evidence

Weigh who produced the evidence and why (Bennett and Checkel, best practice 3):
- Instrumental motives: a source who benefits from an explanation counts for less when supporting it, and more when going against interest.
- Context: spontaneous statements weigh differently from prepared remarks, private from public, and one audience from another.
- Selection: those who control an archive tend to release what flatters them and harms their opponents.
- Secondary sources: historians may choose the sources that confirm them. Check accounts from contending historiographical schools.

## Further checks

- **Every link** (Van Evera). For a chain A → q → r → B, the main hypothesis (A → B), each link, and the mixed combinations can all be tested. Evidence that A and B co-occur does not test the mechanism between them.
- **Tautological predictions** (Van Evera). "We will observe the cause causing its effect" is not a prediction. Predictions should state the incidence, sequence, location or structure of what will be observed, and what decision process, speech or action.
- **Auxiliary outcomes** (Collier, after Mahoney). A cause may leave side effects off the main path, such as the lame sheep in "Silver Blaze". They give extra tests.
- **Hyper-strong tests** (Van Evera). A test run where countervailing forces act, or where the theory's antecedent conditions are missing, is impressive if passed but unfair if failed. Do not count such a failure as a failed hoop.
- **Most and least likely cases** (Bennett and Checkel, best practice 4). Failure in a most-likely case, or success in a least-likely one, should move expectations most. Say whether the text claims either, and whether the case fits.
- **Repairs after contrary evidence** (Bennett and Checkel). The bigger the change made to an explanation to fit contrary evidence, the more new implications it needs to be tested on. Otherwise it becomes a "just so" story that explains away anomalies one at a time.
- **Start and stop** (best practices 5–6). Where the account begins (a critical juncture, the arrival of an actor) and when the search for evidence stops both need a reason.

## Procedure

1. **Claim map.** For each claim: quote it with location; mark whether it is causal, existence or influence; record the confidence the text asserts (its verbs and qualifiers); break it into links.
2. **Rival table.** List the rivals, labelled from the text or from you. Give each one's relationship to the claim, with the step of the decision tree that settles it.
3. **Evidence ledger.** One row for each piece of evidence (format below).
4. **Absences.** Evidence the claim predicts with high certitude that the text does not report. Run the missing-evidence questions on each.
5. **Balance.** For each claim: the strongest tests passed and failed; untested links; rivals tested less hard; whether the stated confidence matches. Name the gap if there is one, for example "asserted as proof, supported by two passed hoops and three straws".
6. **Flags**, each with location, and whether it touches the thesis or is local.
7. **Moves** (Audit, Plan). For each flag: what evidence would raise the test's certitude or uniqueness; which rival to test and how; how to word the claim at the strength its tests allow; what the text should state about certitude and uniqueness. Van Evera: "authors should comment on the uniqueness and certitude of their predictions."

In Plan mode, replace steps 3–5 with a prediction table. For the hypothesis and each rival, list the evidence it predicts and its expected certitude and uniqueness. Mark where the predictions diverge, since that is where the decisive evidence lies. Bennett and Checkel, best practice 9: ask "if my explanation is true, what will be the specific process leading to the outcome?"

## Flags

- **E1 Overclaim.** The stated confidence exceeds what the tests support.
- **E2 Hoop as proof.** Passed hoop tests are presented as confirmation.
- **E3 Shared prediction.** Evidence is offered as support, but the rivals predict it equally well. Watch for "consistent with" without asking what else it is consistent with. Bennett: such evidence may even favour a rival.
- **E4 Assumed exclusivity.** Support for the claim is counted against a rival without establishing mutual exclusivity, or different evidence is treated as divergent. [Zaks]
- **E5 Missing rival.** An explanation the field or the participants would expect goes unaddressed, or a whole category of explanation is absent.
- **E6 Unequal toughness.** The favoured explanation gets "first mover advantage", or the rivals are held to a higher standard.
- **E7 Absence misread.** Missing evidence is treated as evidence of absence without showing it would have been produced and found. Or the reverse: a failed hoop is dismissed with "they would have hidden it", with no argument that they would.
- **E8 Unstated or weak generalisation.** The rule that gives the test its strength is not named, or is only probabilistic while the test is presented as decisive. [Mahoney]
- **E9 Untested link.** The mechanism, or some link in the chain, carries no evidence of its own.
- **E10 Dependent evidence.** Items that follow from one another, or bear on the same rival only, are counted as separate support.
- **E11 Source bias unaddressed.** A source has motives, a context or a selection pattern the text does not weigh.
- **E12 Tautological prediction.** The predicted evidence is simply the claim restated.
- **E13 Ad hoc repair.** Contrary evidence is absorbed by changing the explanation, and no new implications are tested.
- **E14 Ambiguous test type.** The text supports two readings of the evidence's certitude or uniqueness, and nothing in it decides between them. Give both and say what hangs on each. Do not resolve it by guessing.

## Output

Lead with the balance for each claim, then the flags, then the claim map, the rival table and the evidence ledger, then the moves (Audit, Plan). No closing summary. For a long text, put the ledger in a file and give the rest in the reply.

## Don't

- Score claims or texts, or invent probabilities.
- Treat the four types as fixed categories of evidence. They describe a relation between evidence, claim and rivals.
- Assume rivals are mutually exclusive.
- Count a failed hyper-strong test as a failed hoop.
- Present your own knowledge of the field's rival explanations as fact without marking it.
- Apply the tests to interpretive claims about meaning without saying so.
- Rewrite the text. Propose moves; edit only on explicit request.
- Force one test type onto ambiguous evidence.
